ColorMill

ColorMill is a browser-based two-roll mill simulator. A bank of white silicone putty rests on two counter-rotating rollers, is dragged through the nip, sheets onto the front roll and is cut off, rolled into a log, stood over the nip and fed back in end-first. Tap a pigment to drop a chunk of coloured putty onto the bank and watch it disperse. Version 2 runs the whole simulation on the GPU with WebGPU compute (MLS-MPM, up to a million material points), models the material as an elastoplastic putty, and renders it as a ray-marched glossy surface with physically plausible pigment mixing.

Design rule (unchanged since v1): transport happens everywhere, but mixing happens where the material is sheared — at the nip. In v2 that rule is literal: pigment dispersion is driven by the local shear rate, so colours stay crisp in the resting bank and blend in the gap.

Mill layout

The full engineering spec is docs/design-v2.md; this page is the tour. Coordinates are right-handed, +y up, +z toward the viewer, +x to the viewer's right; the roller axes are parallel to x. Sim units are dimensionless (one unit is roughly 0.2 m if you want a physical feel: the roller radius of 0.32 is about a 64 mm roll, and the nip gap is exaggerated so the grid can resolve it).

Both roller surfaces move downward through the nip; the back roll runs faster than the front roll, so the gap shears as well as squeezes.

Front roller

Nearer the viewer. Modelled as a sticky boundary: grid nodes within a tack band (1.5 cells) of its surface take the roller's velocity, normal and tangential, so the sheet is carried around the roll. This is what makes a sheet form at all.

Back roller

Further from the viewer and faster by the friction ratio (1.0–1.6, default 1.25). A separating Coulomb-friction contact: material can leave it. At ratio 1.0 the mill is pure counter-rotation; above it the nip shears.

Bank

The slab of material resting on top of the two rolls. Seeded at start as fresh white silicone (x from 0.05 to 1.45, 0.25 either side of the nip, 0.36 tall above the roll tops), 8 particles per grid cell. It has to hold more material than a gap-thick sheet around the front roll, or the mill consumes it into a ring with no bank.

Nip

The gap between the roll surfaces (0.02–0.10, default 0.04; about 2.6 cells at the high preset). Compression, stretch and shear are strongest here, and it is the only place pigment disperses quickly.

Sheet

The layer that wraps the front roll after the nip, 2–4 cells thick. It is what the operator cuts off and rolls up. A sheet forming within a few seconds of sim time is one of the acceptance checks.

End guides and tray

The domain's x walls model the mill's guide cheeks (no penetration, free slip along the wall). The floor is the tray: a separating wall with stick-slip friction, so spilled material does not skate.

Physics pipeline

The solver is the Material Point Method in its moving-least-squares form (MLS-MPM, Hu et al. 2018) with quadratic B-spline weights and APIC affine velocities, written as WebGPU compute shaders. Particles carry the state; a background grid of nodes (49×41×49 at low up to 121×101×121 at ultra) is scratch space rebuilt every substep. A fixed number of substeps runs per rendered frame, so the simulation is deterministic regardless of frame rate.

1 · Clear

Zero the node accumulators. Mass and momentum are stored as i32 fixed point because WGSL has no float atomics.

2 · P2G

Each particle scatters mass and momentum (plus its stress and affine velocity) to the 27 nodes around it with atomic adds.

3 · Grid

Nodes with mass become velocities; gravity is applied; then the boundaries: front roll (sticky), back roll (Coulomb), walls, floor.

4 · G2P

Particles gather the new velocity and affine matrix, update the deformation gradient, apply the plastic return, advect, and are pushed out of the rolls as a hard safety.

5 · Raster + disperse

Once per frame: pigment latents are rastered to the grid and each particle relaxes toward its neighbourhood in proportion to its shear rate.

6 · Pack

The density and 7-float latent are packed into two rgba16float 3D textures for the renderer.

Elastoplastic putty

Version 1 used a weakly compressible fluid, which is why it read as a puddle: a fluid cannot hold a bank on top of two rolls. Uncured silicone is a putty — it keeps its shape under gravity, yields under the nip's shear, and does not spring back. v2 uses the fixed-corotated elastic model (Stomakhin et al. 2013) with a deviatoric-only plastic return: every step the shape part of the deformation gradient is clamped to a small yield band ([1 − 0.025, 1 + 0.0075]) while the volume part is kept, so the pressure term keeps resisting compression. (Clamping the raw singular values, as the snow model does, lets the nip pack the putty to several times rest density and swallow the bank.) Young's modulus 15 in unit-density sim units — soft enough that the bank slumps into a rolling bank and keeps the nip fed, which is what makes the sheet continuous — Poisson ratio 0.47, with a short viscoplastic relaxation so yielded material keeps flowing under sustained shear instead of locking into a shear band; the substep length per preset follows from the CFL bound with a safety factor of 4.

Sticky and separating rollers, friction ratio

The rolls are analytic cylinders applied as grid boundary conditions, and they are deliberately different. The front roll takes full control of nodes inside its tack band (the sheet follows the roll). The back roll removes the approaching normal velocity and applies Coulomb friction to the tangential part (μ = 0.4), so material can leave it. Because the back roll is faster, the nip is a shear zone: the sheet ends up on the front (slower, tackier) roll, as on a real mill.

Shear-driven dispersion in Mixbox latent space

Every particle carries a 7-float Mixbox latent (three Kubelka–Munk pigment weights plus a residual). Latents are linear under mass-weighted averaging, so transport, rastering and blending are plain arithmetic, and the polynomial that turns a latent into RGB is evaluated once per pixel in the render shader. Mixing is not diffusion: once per frame each particle moves its latent toward the node-averaged latent around it by k · γ · dt, where γ is the local shear rate read off the particle's own APIC affine matrix. In the resting bank γ ≈ 0 and colours stay crisp; in the nip they blend. Blue and yellow fold into green because the mixing is done in pigment space, not RGB.

Cut & fold

The move most milling actually uses: hold a knife against the sheet on the front face of the front roll and draw it from the roll end toward the middle while the sheet comes up past it. Because the sheet moves during the stroke the cut is a diagonal, and the freed flap is a triangle, pointed at the top near the roll end and wide at the bottom where the sheet leaves the nip. The operator takes the bottom corner and folds the flap over the cut onto the sheet beside it, toward the middle, roll side up, and the doubled sheet rides up into the nip. The script mirrors each particle across the cut line in the sheet's own coordinates, lands it outside the sheet there by the flap's thickness (or on the bank top where the image reaches back over the crown), and gets it there as a page turning on the cut, lifted in proportion to its distance from the hinge. Ends alternate; each fold doubles a wedge of the sheet toward the middle.

Cut & roll

A two-roll mill has no axial transport; the operator cuts the sheet off the front roll, rolls it into a log, turns the log 90° and feeds it back in end-on, and that is where lateral mixing comes from. v2 scripts the move: everything on the mill (sheet, bank and all) is marked kinematic, folded in half across its width and wound into a volume-preserving spiral of varying thickness (a mass histogram along the arc; the bank is kneaded flat into the core, the sheet wraps around it) standing tilted over the nip (1.2 s); the log is then fed down and released only onto the pile the nip is actually eating, so the rolls consume it end-first over a few seconds and nothing drips in mid-air. No material is left behind or packed denser than it was; material from every position along the roll ends up in the log's cross-section and is spread back out across the width as the nip flattens it.

Rendering

A single fullscreen ray-march pass walks the density volume, finds the iso-surface, takes the normal from the density gradient, decodes the latent at the hit into an albedo and shades it with a key, fill and rim light, a two-lobe specular (silicone putty is glossy) and a cheap density-based ambient occlusion that darkens creases and the nip. The rolls (brushed metal with a rotating stripe so speed is visible) and the tray are analytic and depth-composited, so they stay crisp at every grid resolution.

Only dt and the substep count change between presets; the material constants are the same, so the mill behaves the same at every quality. Simulated time per frame is dt × substeps (about 13 ms at high): at 60 fps the mill runs at roughly 0.8× real time, and the HUD shows the actual ratio. The app picks medium on non-discrete GPUs (low on mobile) and high otherwise.

Acceptance bar

v2 is done when, in headless Chromium at the low preset: 200 frames run with no NaN/Inf, every particle inside the domain and outside both rolls, and the particle count unchanged; a sheet forms on the front roll within 3 s of sim time while the back roll carries less than a third as much; injecting blue and yellow and running 6 s produces green voxels in the sheet; the rendered frame shows a smooth lit surface; and npm run build, npm test and npm run test:e2e all pass.

Where next

  1. Calibration. Match the sheet thickness, bank shape and number of passes to homogenise against a real lab mill and real silicone.
  2. Operator model. Replace the scripted fold with an interactive knife and a bank you can push with a finger.
  3. Optics. Translucent and transparent bases (silicone is rarely opaque white), pigment strength, and thin-sheet transmission.
  4. Saved runs. Reproducible scenarios with settings and screenshots for review.
